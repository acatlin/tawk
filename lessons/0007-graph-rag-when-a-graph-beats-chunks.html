Graph RAG: when a graph beats chunks

A graph index pays for itself on relational and corpus-wide questions, and costs you dearly everywhere else. This lesson teaches you to tell which questions yours are.

Time 30 minutes Builds on Lesson 5 (approved sources) Win A written decision on whether your corpus needs a graph, and a prototype if it does
By the end you can
  • Define Graph RAG in one sentence and name the two retrieval modes that matter (local and global).
  • Describe Microsoft GraphRAG’s indexing pipeline and say where the cost goes.
  • Quote three independent measurements about when graphs help and when they do not.
  • Prototype a k-hop graph retrieval in TypeScript and compare it with chunk retrieval on the same question.

Why this matters for your bot

Your corpus is small and curated, so you will probably not need a graph on day one. You asked to understand Graph RAG anyway, and that is the right instinct: the questions a FAQ and chunk retrieval get wrong tend to be relational (“I’m on the plan with the e-bike discount; which support line do I call?”) or corpus-wide (“what are the main reasons people contact you?”). Knowing what a graph buys you, and what it costs, lets you recognise those failures in your transcripts and respond proportionately instead of rebuilding your stack.

One sentence, two retrieval modes

Neo4j’s definition is the cleanest: “GraphRAG is RAG, where the Retrieval path includes a knowledge graph.”The GraphRAG manifesto (Neo4j, vendor) Instead of retrieving only text chunks that look like the question, you also retrieve structure: entities, the relationships between them, and summaries of the clusters they form.

Microsoft’s GraphRAG, the reference implementation most people mean, offers several query modes. Two carry the idea:Microsoft GraphRAG docs: query overview

  • Local search combines “relevant data from the AI-extracted knowledge-graph with text chunks” and suits “questions that require an understanding of specific entities mentioned in the documents.” This is the multi-hop case.
  • Global search runs “over all AI-generated community reports in a map-reduce fashion” for “questions that require an understanding of the dataset as a whole.” This is the sensemaking case.

The docs also list DRIFT search (local search refined with community information) and Basic search, “a rudimentary implementation of basic vector RAG” kept for comparison.Microsoft GraphRAG docs: query overview

How the index is built, and where the cost goes

Microsoft says so plainly: the getting-started page warns “GraphRAG can consume a lot of LLM resources!” and suggests experimenting with inexpensive models first,Microsoft GraphRAG docs: get started and the repository README states that indexing “can be an expensive operation” and that the project is “largely in maintenance mode.”microsoft/graphrag repository The same authors later published LazyGraphRAG, whose indexing cost they put at “0.1% of the costs of full GraphRAG.”LazyGraphRAG (Microsoft Research blog, vendor)

What independent measurements say

The original paper reports large wins for global “sensemaking” questions over corpora of about a million tokens: comprehensiveness win rates of 72–83% against vector RAG, and root-level community summaries needing “over 97% fewer tokens” than source text.Edge et al., “From Local to Global” (arXiv 2404.16130) That is the use case it was designed for. The rule that falls out

Chunks for facts. Graphs for relations and themes. And keep the honest “not in my sources” behaviour from lesson 5 regardless, because the global mode in particular is prone to answering questions that have no answer.

Decide for your corpus

Write the decision down; it is a learning record for your project. Add a graph index only when at least one of these is true:

  1. Transcripts show multi-hop failures. Visitors ask questions that need two facts joined across pages, and the chunk tier retrieves one page but not the bridge. (Lesson 3’s webhook transcripts are where you will see this.)
  2. You need corpus-wide answers. “What are the main themes / most common issues / everything that changed this quarter.” That is global search territory.
  3. You need explainable paths or graph-level permissions. A traversal is inspectable in a way a cosine score is not; Neo4j’s manifesto argues this case at length (as a vendor).The GraphRAG manifesto (Neo4j, vendor)

If none is true, there is an even simpler option than retrieval for a corpus your size: Anthropic’s contextual retrieval write-up notes that when a knowledge base is “smaller than 200,000 tokens” (about 500 pages) you can put the whole thing in the prompt and rely on prompt caching.Anthropic: Introducing Contextual Retrieval Two help sites and a FAQ will often fit.

Prototype: a graph you can read in one sitting

A knowledge graph can be a list of triples with provenance. Retrieval is “find the entities named in the question, walk k hops, hand the walked triples to the model.” That is local search with the machinery removed, and it is enough to feel the difference. The tested module is examples/src/graph-lite.ts:

import { GraphIndex, parseTriples } from "./graph-lite.js";

const graph = new GraphIndex(parseTriples(`
Annual Membership | includes | E-bike discount      | plans-page
Annual Membership | includes | Priority phone line  | support-page
Monthly Membership | includes | Email support       | support-page
E-bike discount   | valid at | Riverside Station    | plans-page
`));

const question = "I'm on the plan with the e-bike discount; which support channel do I get?";
const seeds = graph.findEntities(question);          // ["E-bike discount"]
const hood  = graph.neighborhood(seeds, 2);          // 2 hops: discount → plan → support line
console.log(GraphIndex.toContext(hood.triples));
// - Annual Membership — includes — E-bike discount [plans-page]
// - Annual Membership — includes — Priority phone line [support-page]
// - E-bike discount — valid at — Riverside Station [plans-page]

Two design points carry over to any real system:

  • Keep provenance on every triple. The source field is how you get back to a citable chunk, so graph context never breaks the citation rule from lesson 5.
  • Extraction is the expensive, lossy step. In the systematic evaluation “only about 65.8% of answer entities appear in the constructed KG” for one benchmark.Han et al., “RAG vs. GraphRAG” (arXiv 2502.11371) If an entity was never extracted, no traversal will find it. Use a fixed schema (entity types, allowed predicates) when you ask Claude to extract triples; Anthropic publishes a cookbook on knowledge-graph construction with Claude.Claude cookbook: knowledge graph construction

Primary sources to read

Read the abstract and Section 5 of “From Local to Global” to see what Graph RAG was built for, then the findings section of GraphRAG-Bench to see where it stops helping. Together they take under an hour and replace most blog posts on the subject.

Carry it into your project

  • Decision recorded in your repo: graph now, graph later with a named trigger, or no graph. One paragraph with the evidence.
  • A saved search over transcripts for multi-hop phrasings (“the plan that…”, “which … do I get if…”).
  • If prototyping: a fixed extraction schema (entity types, allowed predicates) and provenance on every triple.
  • The NOT_IN_SOURCES rule still applies to graph context.
